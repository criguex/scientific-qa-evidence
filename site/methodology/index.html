A theorem is a Given and a Then.

In software QA, before a single line of the program is written, you write down what must hold: Given a context, When something happens, Then a result can be observed. This page takes the Lean proof OpenAI published on Navier–Stokes and shows how it is audited with that same method: user stories, yes-or-no criteria, scenarios, test cases, executions and evidence.

Scenario: (C) the fluid breaks down on ℝ³
  Given a viscosity ν greater than 0

  And both are smooth and decay
    away from the origin
  Then there is no solution (v, p)
    that is smooth for all time
    with bounded energy

Gherkin is not a logic: it has no quantifiers and no formal negation. It lets a person read the intent. The machine checks the Lean; a person checks that the Lean says what the sentence promises. That difference is the heart of this audit.

Chapter 1

The language of QA, translated into mathematics

Each QA word has a close relative in mathematical work. They are not the same, and the differences are where it gets interesting: a theorem is proved once and holds forever, while a program changes every week and has to be tested again. That is why QA lives on repeatable evidence.

User story (US)

What it is
A need told from the side of whoever has it: as a <role>, I want <something>, so that <a purpose>.
In mathematics
The motivation of a problem: why anyone cares about this statement.
Here
"As the mathematical community, I want to know whether the Lean statement is Clay's question."

Acceptance criterion (AC)

What it is
A question that only allows yes or no and decides whether the story is met. If evidence cannot answer it, it is not a criterion.
In mathematics
A condition that must be checked before accepting the conclusion.
Here
"Does the checker reject every one-symbol change to the statement?" Yes, 6 of 6.

Scenario (Gherkin)

What it is
The criterion written in natural language with fixed words: Given, When, Then, And, But.
In mathematics
Hypotheses, construction and thesis. "Let… take… then…".
Here
Given commit f9e8bc5, When I build it, Then it exits with code 0.

Outline and Examples

What it is
A scenario with blanks (<trap>) and a table of values. Each row produces one concrete scenario.
In mathematics
A "for all" over a finite, explicit set. It is not the ∀ of a theorem: it only covers the rows written down.
Here
Six traps in a single template.

Test case (TC)

What it is
The concrete action that answers a criterion: what runs, with what data, and what is expected.
In mathematics
A worked example, or the check of a particular case.
Here
Build without cache and check that every .olean file is new.

Execution and evidence

What it is
A real run of the case, with date, version, command, exit code and a record someone else can review.
In mathematics
The certificate: something that can be checked again without trusting whoever produced it.
Here
B4, 21 October, 1 h 29 min, exit code 0, log with its hash.

Mutant and control

What it is
A mutant is an error planted on purpose to see whether the test catches it. A control is a harmless change the test must let through.
In mathematics
Perturbing a hypothesis to see whether the argument uses it; the control is the trivial case that must not break anything.
Here
Changing ν > 0 to ν ≥ 0, or only a comment.

Defect (bug)

What it is
A criterion something promised to meet and did not. It is not for winning an argument: it is for finding the cause and fixing it.
In mathematics
An error in a step, or a hypothesis used without saying so.
Here
Three, all three in my own work, fixed in public.

Traceability

What it is
Being able to go from the story to the criterion, the scenario, the case, the execution and the evidence, and back, with no gaps.
In mathematics
Every claim in the paper has its lemma and every lemma its proof.
Here
The matrix at the end: 24 rows, one per criterion.

Anatomy of a scenario

Gherkin has few words, and each one has a fixed role. The official Gherkin parser, the same one testing tools use, classifies every step as context, action, outcome or conjunction. Tap a line of the scenario to see what it does.

Scenario outline

One template, six scenarios

The outline above has one blank, <trap>. Each row of the Examples table fills it and produces a complete scenario. It is a "for all" over six explicit cases, not over every possible change. That is why, besides the traps, we need to understand what a rejection means (chapter US-3).

Chapter 3

Verifying, testing the test and validating are not the same

Think of a cake. Verifying is checking that the recipe was followed step by step. Validating is checking that it was the cake the customer ordered. You can follow a recipe perfectly and deliver the wrong cake. Between the two there is a third step that mathematics rarely names: testing the test, that is, checking that the checker really checks.

The machine

checks the computation

verify

Lean checks every step and Comparator compares the statement with the challenge's. If a step fails, it does not build. It answers: is the proof correct for this statement?

The traps

check the machine

test the test

Change one symbol of the statement and see whether the checker notices; change a comment and see that it does not complain. It answers: is the checker exact, or does it let anything through?

A person

checks the question

validate

Someone who knows the problem reads the Lean statement next to Clay's text. No machine can do this alone. It answers: is it the question we wanted to answer?

Chapter 4

The method, in seven steps

  1. 1

    Understand what is asked

    The need is written as a user story: who has it, what they want and why.

    In this audit: six stories, from "it builds" to "someone else repeats it without me".

  2. 2

    Analyze before testing

    Ask what could go wrong and rank the risks by impact. I am not a test executor: first I am an analyst.

    Main risk: that the verified statement is not the official question, for example an easier version.

  3. 3

    Write the acceptance criteria

    Each risk becomes a yes-or-no question that evidence can answer.

    24 criteria. Without evidence, the answer is no.

  4. 4

    Write the scenarios in Gherkin

    Each criterion is written with Given, When and Then before anything runs. That way nobody adjusts the expectation to the result.

    Six .feature files, one per story, validated with the official parser.

  5. 5

    Design the test cases

    Each scenario becomes concrete actions: build, run a checker, plant an error.

    Builds with and without cache, 6 traps and 1 control, 11 Mathlib controls, one fingerprint on five systems.

  6. 6

    Run and keep the evidence

    Each run leaves its command, version, hash and exit code, failed runs included.

    Builds B1 to B5, a cloud rebuild, a campaign on three Lean releases and every log with its SHA-256.

  7. 7

    Decide and report

    Each criterion ends as yes or no, with its evidence. Defects are recorded, my own included, and what is missing is said plainly.

    20 of 24 criteria at yes. 4 defects, all mine. 0 in OpenAI's proof.

Chapter 6

Defects found

An audit that only finds other people's errors is suspicious. These four defects were mine, and the same audit caught them in later steps. The first three were fixed in public; the fourth was documented without touching the published files, so the fingerprint does not change. They are findings 1, 3, 8 and 9 in FINDINGS.md.

D-1 · finding 1

A "clean" build was not clean

What happened
Build B3 had received 7 small dependencies already compiled (399 modules). The build log did not show it.
How it was found
By comparing each .olean file's timestamp with the build start, and inspecting the package folder.
Fix
B4 and B5 compile everything on the machine, with no cache.
D-2 · finding 3

"The same build" was two builds

What happened
Two runs reported as using the same build had used different builds.
How it was found
With per-run receipts and the original hash of each file.
Fix
Every run now says which build it used.
D-3 · finding 8

The tool said "valid" when it failed

What happened
The numerical runner reported oracle_valid: true even when the oracle had raised an exception or returned NaN.
How it was found
By testing the tool itself: failures were injected on purpose.
Fix
It now marks INCONCLUSIVE and, if export fails, exits with code 2.
D-4 · finding 9

Three published traps did not apply

What happened
Three of the seven published mutant diffs (domain_R3_to_periodic_data, force_zero_R3 and force_zero_periodic) lack their last context line, a single space. As published they do not apply: git apply stops with "corrupt patch".
How it was found
By rerunning the traps in the version campaign on 24 Nov. With the line restored, each diff's SHA-256 matches the original recorded in PROVENANCE.md: the original runs used complete diffs.
Status
Documented. The restored copies are in experiments/version-port/R0/diffs/. The published ones are left unchanged, because changing them would change the data version and the fingerprint.
0

Defects found in OpenAI's proofs, within this scope. Zero defects is not a certification: it is what these tests saw.

Chapter 9

Questions to think about

For discussion at the seminar. Try to answer before opening each one.

If Comparator rejects the statement with f = 0, does that prove anything about the problem without external force?

No. Comparator compares statements; it neither proves nor refutes them. The rejection only says "this is not the challenge statement". Clay's alternatives (A) and (B), without external force, remain open, and this audit says nothing about them.

Why have a control that is accepted, if what we look for are rejections?

Without the control you cannot tell an exact checker from one that rejects everything. If it also rejected a comment change, the six rejections would mean nothing: it would reject for any reason. The negative control calibrates the test.

Comparator rejected equivalent changes, such as n + 1 ≤ p instead of n < p. Is that a defect?

No: it shows what it compares. Comparator compares the form of the statement, not its meaning. So a rejection means "changed", not "false", and telling equivalent, weaker and false apart takes a second Lean proof for each case. That was done with three Mathlib theorems: the infinitude of primes, Cauchy–Schwarz and dominated convergence.

Two separately written checkers accept the proof. Does that guarantee it is correct?

It lowers the chance of an error a lot, but does not remove it: both could share a bug nobody knows yet. That is why it was also checked that both include every known kernel soundness fix, and a third one was attempted.

Why does periodic pressure matter in (D) and not in (C)?

(C) and (D) say "there exist data such that no solution exists". Asking more of the solution (that p be periodic) shrinks the class of solutions, and "none exists" becomes easier to prove. With u and f periodic, the equation forces ∇p to be periodic, but p may carry a linear part c(t)·x. If the erratum does not require p periodic, the formal (D) would be weaker than Clay's. (C) lives on ℝ³ and has no such condition.

If I edit the README, does the fingerprint change?

No. The fingerprint depends on the data version, and that version is computed only from the evidence, experiment and bridge files. The README does have its hash in the manifest, so the first check detects if someone changed it, but the data version, and with it the fingerprint, does not move. You can try it in the US-5 calculator.

What would change if my Clay transcription had an error?

AC-4.2 would still say yes, because OpenAI's theorems would still imply what I wrote. But what I wrote would no longer be Clay. That is why AC-4.3 exists and says no: the chain is as strong as its human link.
